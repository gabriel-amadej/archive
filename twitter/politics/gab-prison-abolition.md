“addendum: i was wrong, but i almost had a point” aside, this is richly illustrative of the sickening conceit looming behind lazy anti-abolitionism. prison abolitionists, in actual fact, have exhaustive and detailed responses to common concerns. so much so that it's almost boring to go through the motions, as we can cognitively model every possible conversation on the matter. you were confusing prison abolitionism *as such* with communists who pick up the label for 2 years in their teenage lives. more matured prison abolitionists get sick of their often essay-length responses predictably having no impact on the vengeful mobs. ('vengeful' no matter their proclaimed theoretical motivations, such as self-styled anti-retributivists): which, of course, reveals the personal vices of those who oppose prison abolitionism. 

when it comes down to it, most people cruelly and animalistically want others to suffer, even when they reject retributive theories of just imprisonment, and that is a fatal flaw which is hard to dispel with the ample empirical and moral-dialogical evidence in our favour. 

anyway, the general dialogical flow is this.

a] set a moral precedent against motivations like punishment, deterrence, and, for more even radical abolitionists, restoration. one attractive view is that rights like bodily autonomy have such high presumptions in their favour that kidnapping and imprisoning a person for a wrongdoing is simply an inexcusable rights violation. such acts tend to only be justifiable in the in case of temporary restraint, i.e. to do things like disarm an immediate threat or establish boundaries between a stalker and a victim. 
you can makeshift 'arrest' someone to dissolve a domestic abuse situation, but you can't lock them inside a deeply existential torture factory.

b] set out the empirical case that most violent crimes are one-off crimes of passion. 

and even serial rapists and serial killers are distinct from people who will truly never stop. if our baseline concern is the stability of society — often the concern if step a] is successful — then we only have a few, very rare 'Genghis Khan' cases. who can thus be executed, 
either by a third-party or one of their inevitable victims in self-defence. thus, either collective or individual self-defence justifies this action for rare and unignorable criminals. this often comes with the moral case that non-tortuous and non-retributive death is better than 
life in prison. (e.g. rituals like victims watching criminals die of painful and frightening potassium overdoses are moraly disallowed.) moreover, prisons, like all institutions, seek to reproduce themselves and expand their power. injustice *inevitably* grows out of prison. 

meaning that the ideal prison system you can imagine a priori will always, given sufficient time, evolve into torture factories that disproportionately harm people under almost every theory of justice. 

c] lay out intuition pumps for what a non-neurotic culture of radical forgiveness would look like, weaving in the spiritual and character benefits of not seeing crime as something to, with authoritarian conceit, 'solve,' but accept and expect to decrease as the aggregate wealth 
of society improves and our social incentive structures become ever more complex. there is a reason that urban cities in the first world have been steadily decreasing in objective, as opposed to perceived crime rates, 
and it has nothing to do with a criminal gang licensed by the state.

this is, of course, a non-exhaustive listing of how the approach needs to go.

you don't need institutional responses to most, if any crimes. social norms in well-developed societies are enough. 
even the smallest acts, like a disapproving look, are the real constituents of social order. (*coughs* read Giddens and Goffman.) once the moral prejudices in favour of things like retribution are (easily) swept aside, 
it all becomes a matter of explaining why prison abolitionism wouldn't lead to aggregate chaos or at least a meaningful net increase in crime. which is, in fact, very easy to do.
