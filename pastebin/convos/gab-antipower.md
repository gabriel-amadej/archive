#### Me:

It is pretty good overall. His conception of antipower makes for a pretty robust and useful conception of liberty in turn. I 
especially agree with several of its implications, such as that (1) non-interference on part of a person with the ability to 
restrict your options or harm you with relative or absolute impunity does not make it a free relationship, (2) relationships that 
once had a voluntary origin are not necessarily still voluntary at a later time, and (3) non-violent relationships between e.g. 
standard employees/bosses or husbands/wives that are dominating still represent a genuine loss of liberty. (Though I'm not 
inclined to agree with his point in 2. that historical violence is irrelevant to whether a present relationship is unfree.) 
Furthermore, the fact that it allows us to cut across the silly negative/positive liberty distinction is only a positive for 
conceiving of freedom as antipower.

.

One thing that was silly was when he had a footnote acknowledging his statement that institutions of civil society are probably 
more effective and useful for creating conditions of equal antipower than legalistic and statist ones could be turned into a 
general antistatist argument, wherein he said he doesn't really have much faith in such an argument. I find this a little odd 
since he mentioned several times in the beginning that successful constitutional restraints on the judiciary and legislature, and 
non-abusive enforcement of its law, is tantamount to a miracle. Their argument that a top-down power structure as constitutionally 
restrained does not /necesarily/ have the capacities to restrain people in a subjugating manner is also pretty unimpressive. For 
one, their own point that such an idealistic state justice system is miraculous seems to undermine it -- it is, as a empirical 
fact, so extremely unlikely to be a reality that its simple existence is a practical threat to the freedom of people in the exact 
way he expresses worry about. At first glance, maybe he can write this off as being a virtual capacity for subjugation not an 
actual one, but that's not really the case. For he already mentions that the government institutions themselves have the power to 
restrain, and it is ideal constitutional restraints that limits them in such a way that they are non-subjugating. So it is 
effectively actual until the point in time the miracle takes effect.

.

Lastly, and it is hard to nail down their exact position on this, I don't think their conception of freedom as antipower is so 
much opposed to freedom as non-interference as much as it is a mature, extended version of it -- a reverse parallel to how, 
historically he argues, freedom as non-interference was a limited and conservative interpretation of this more traditional 
conception. I am not really sure where in their conception of freedom that 'non-subjugating coercion' is even a coherent thing, 
much less not counting as a negation of liberty.

.

#### Jason:

I largely agree.

.

I feel like he also equivocates a lot when talking about non-interference between Hobbesian views (which treat all interference, 
even defense, as morally identical), and libertarian ones (which only count aggressive interference as morally objectionable.)

.

Furthermore, much of what he stresses about the nature of slavery seems to be about the fact that the slaveholder claims to /own/ 
the slave, not just that they dominate them in a more general sense.

.

Which would mean that self-ownership / self-sovereignty / whatever style libertarianism best accounts for the specific fundamental 
wrong thing about slavery.

.

Also, with that in mind, his discussion about anti-power as a kind of positive thing itself beyond just the absence of power made 
me think that perhaps it's useful to think of property as a paradigmatic case of anti-power.

.

And then pulling back to the more general domination stuff, I think a good way of formalizing the relationship between the "thick" 
aspects of libertarianism (i.e., the "left" aspects of "left-libertarianism") and the "thin" ones might be found in a synthesis of 
neo-republican (non-domination) and standard libertarian (non-aggression / self-ownership) views of freedom.

.

Finally, I have no clue how you get a justification for political authority out of all of this.

.

It seems like any and all claims to political authority are going to involve domination. The rule of law stuff doesn't do much for 
that, because if taken seriously, that seems to get you to requiring a polycentric constitution (ie, anarchy.)

.

If you just take it to mean having a binding constitution, that still gives the organization of government the ability to 
interfere pretty arbitrarily, even if it has to do so through really formal processes.

.

Which leads to another more general issue.

.

Which is that multiple times he seems to say that a given relationship can be necessarily dominating, but okay if there's some 
regulatory force checking it. This is odd.

.

Though I don't think he ever says that directly, he seems to say things that amount to that.

.
