Idealism, broadly defined, is a theory about the external world that holds that no physical objects exist. Traditionally, it has been thought as the doctrine that only ideal objects exist, and that no non-ideal objects exist. While this definition works in some cases, the forms of idealism that evolved in the 19th and, to a smaller extent, the 20th century make this traditional definition invalid. The two tenants of idealism are therefore:

(i) Physical objects do not exist.

(ii) Mental objects exist, which are all or mostly responsible for making up the constituents of reality in some sense.

All forms of idealism are a monist ontology in that they hold only once substance exists, though this need not preclude non-ideal abstract objects, because they are not bone-fide substance.

Please note that some individual philosophers are apt to break or stretch these boundaries, so this criteria is not exactly rigorous. One thing remains certain however: it is obvious to anyone that the self-titled brands of idealism in the academic world tend to fit the profile of idealism.

.

An ideal object is a mental object, though some philosophers have referred to these as mind-dependent objects. The problem with this definition is that mind-dependency implies that an object is dependent on the attitudes or perception of a continuos mind; a subject. The mental is not to be confused with the mind, for mind implies this continuity, and on the worst interpretations, a person behind it. The mental is best defined as phenomenal content. Phenomenal content can refer to and encompass one or more of the following mental phenomena:

-Qualia, e.g. the experience of feeling, what it is like to be, the experience of colour e.g. the experience of redness, etc etc.

-Conceptual thought.

-Personhood.

-Attitudes, e.g. having propositional attitudes ("it seems to me that P"), being angry towards oneself or toward another, etc etc.

-Physiological continuity (forms the basis of neo-lockean conceptions of personhood).

-Awareness, e.g. of surrondings, thoughts, self, qualia, attitudes, etc etc.

.

You may have noticed, either by reading through that list, or in your day-to-day life, that qualia encompasses all of these things. The important thing to keep in mind is that qualia need not a *necessarily* encompass all of these. A healthy human or human-equivalent person will have all of the contents of phenomenal experience, while earthly animals seem to possess qualia, nonpropositional attitudes,  and some aspects of awareness.

What we refer to as sentients have the capacity for phenomenal experience that include at least some of the phenomenal contents listed above.

To no surprise of anyone, a non-ideal object is therefore a non-mental object. Some examples of non-ideal objects would be physical objects and objective abstract objects.

.

#### Classical idealism:

This was the first formulation if idealism, and it attempted to ground all of reality in the mental. It does, however, allow for abstract objects to exist independently from subjects as ideal objects. What distinguishes classical idealism from other forms idealism is that it held that "Mind"--to be distinguished from simple phenomenal experience--grounds all reality; that from which all things in reality exist within.

.

#### Subjective idealism:

Subjective idealism is aptly summarized by the following statement by the Bishop, Bekeley: "To be is to be perceived." Subjective idealism holds that reality is nothing more than a collection of sense data in the perceiver. Another form of subjective idealism holds that there is a singular, absolute world image, which is being projected by some absolute world-mind or cosmic overmind. It contends that an infinite number of individual minds are participating.

.

#### Transcendal idealism:

Transcendal idealism is not so much bone-fide idealism as it is a form of a representationalist theory of perceptual experience in its growing pains. Contemporary notions of representationalism hold that we see mental images of external objects, and that we are not directly aware of the objects themselves; we are indirectly aware. Kant was the originator for transcdental idealism, and he held that space and time are intuitions, so that our minds actually shape the world we perceive. He distinguishes between objects themselves and what we perceive. He says that we cannot actually access objects in-of-themselves, and we have to rely on our synthetic, a priori spacial intuitions that form our perceptual experience to know them indirectly.

#### Objective idealism:

Holds that the reality of experiencing transcends yet combines the realities of any given object experienced and the mind of a subject.

.

#### Absolute idealism:

Absolute idealism can be attributed to Hegel. His idea is that being is an all inclusive whole. That, in order for subject to relate to object, there must some point of identity between the two. Hegel's worry was that otherwise we would have no access to object, and thus we wouldn't know anything about the world. This point of identity is the basis of all concepts from which we can make sense of reality.

.

Before we go any further, we must identity three theories that, although not directly forms of idealism, have close relationships to them and are born out of the mind/body debackle. They are three theories of how the mental could be fundamental rather than emergent.

#### Panpsychism:

The view that all objects have a unified phenomenological experience.

.

#### Panexperientialism:

This view holds that all non-subject enttites have a respectable degree of phenomenological experience, but they are not full-blown minds, so they lack cognition.

.

#### Panprotoexperientialism:

This view credits all non-subject entities with latent, undeveloped phenomenological experience. They lack any kind of conscious awareness and cognition.

.

These theories can actually be interwined with different variations of idealism to bring abought something new.

#### Actual idealism:

This is a positon that tries to formulate a more grounded idealism that contrasts Kant's Transcendal Idealism and Hegel's Absolute Idealism.

.

#### Pluralistic idealism:

Pluralistic idealism generally holds that there are many minds underlying the existence of the observable world, who make possible the existence of the universe.

_~Leibniz' pluralistic idealism:_

There exist what we may call monads--substantive forms of being--that are the actual atoms of the universe, and who are entities capable of perception. They are individual, non-interactive, and each of them reflect the entire universe. These monads would be the center of substance. However, things like space are phenomenal in nature, and rely on the monads. The central monad is God, who harmonizes the relationship between external objects and the minds of monads.

_~Personalism:_

This holds that the minds underlying reality are persons. Reality is a society of people dependent on God.

_~McTaggard's variation:_

McTaggard argued that only minds exist, and we only relate to each other through love.

.

#### Panpsychist Absolute Idealism:

This viewed was developed by the philosopher Timothy Sprigge. He contrasts panpsychicsm with absolute idealism, in where reality is comprised of bits of experience combined into a certain whole. Given the nature of panphysicsm, and the nature of his other writings, it is reasonable to assume that this coherent whole would be properly called the mind of God. It is however necessary to note that this brand of idealism need not be theistic, it is just the case that its only proponent was a theist.
