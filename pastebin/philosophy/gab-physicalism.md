#### (1)

Let us consider the mental state of pain. For us humans, this is realizable through c-fiber receptors firing off. However, there is an entirely different physical function in animals that causally explain their experience of pain. Additionally, you can consider the functions of the nervous systems in plants that make-up "pain" in a non-experiential sense. It is perfectly conceivable that if a mind were to be infused within a plant, or if a plant had the sufficient capacities, then it be able to causally deliver the experience of pain for that phenomenal subject who happens to be a plant. This is what it means for mental states to be multiply-realizable from non-mental states. For any mental state M, there can exist non-mental states N, N1, N2, etc. to ad infinitum, that are equally able to given rise to M. Therefore, M is not equivalent to any N.

#### (2)

If you were to hook a subject up to some machine or another so that you were able to scan the neuroactivity in their brain, then you could look for what brain states arise and disemerge when you cause them something like pain. To do this, you could put a fair sized needle in them deep enough so that it causes at least a moderate amount of pain - then you can examine the neurophysiological activity. You will find a change in brain states, yes, but all you will ever be looking at are functions, that, at best can be said to causally be responsible for the subjects experiencing of pain. You will not find the contents of the mental states i.e. the qualia anywhere. If you were looking at the test as a phenomenologically void p-zombie, and had no reports from anyone else that such a thing like the inner subjective experience existed, then you would thinking nothing more than those brain states as observed. You would not be able to infer that they were having any qualia at all. You would think of pain in terms of physical function, and nothing more. What physicalists (the ones that are not eliminativists) tend to do is introspect on their mental states to truly know them, then upon seeing brain functions causally being responsible for experience via function, they fallacious confuse that with being the content of the mental states.

#### (3)

Now, this is an argument that will have an obvious objection to it, but don't worry, I will explain why it is no real objection at all. The argument is simple, and it goes as follows: I can conceive of myself existing independent of my brain and body, and the experience of my mental states being there without any brain states being causally responsible for them, so what is true of my brain must not necessarily be true of my mind.

The objection is that this applies to everything. You can conceive of life existing without DNA. You can conceive of water existing without H20 etc etc. The reply? Well, yes you can, which means that it is at least metaphysically possible. We have discovered through empirical means that the chemical composition of what we refer to as water is H20, but this does not mean that water is ontologically reducible to H20. Water != H20, but it is true that where there is water we will find H20. So yes, it is metaphysically possible that life could exist without DNA, and that water could without H20, but in the actual world, it doesn't. However, this still carries the same conclusion, which also does not commit us to having to say that we can find water without H20 in our world: what is true of H20 is not what is true of water. They are ontologically distinct in all possible worlds, though they are functionally linked in our world.

This does leave room for the rejection of substance dualism (i.e. the traditional notion of a soul), but it does make impossible reductive forms of physicalism. If this argument works, then the most materalist-esque conclusion you can get is that of non-reductive physicalism. This view would hold that mental states are supervenient upon physical ones, but nonetheless they are ontologically distinct. So, while this does not get you "the soul", or mind as a bone fide substance, it does give you irreducible mental states.

#### (4)

This is known as the Mary's Room Argument. For thought experiment experiment purposes, let us say there exists a colourblind neuroscientist. She has lived her whole life in a monochrome environment. Throughout here life, she learns all the physical facts there is to know about colour and things related. She learns about wavelength of light, the brain, etc etc. When she is released, she gets surgery to repair her sight so that she is able to see colour once again. When she gazes upon a blue sky for the very first time, it seems that she would learn something new about colour that she didn't know beforehand, even though she knew all the physical facts about colour. In particular, she come to know the experience of blue, or in other terms, what blueness is like. This means that there are non-physical facts about experience that physicalist ontologies cannot seem to account for. It should be noted that whether or not she is able to use visual tricks to perceive colour in the monochrome environment is irrelevant. What the point of the thought experiment is that, independent of the physical facts about colour, there are non-physical facts she would learn upon seeing something like red for the first time - even though she has all the physical facts beforehand. The only plausible objection I can see to this is to try to say that knowledge cannot be nonpropositional, in which case you the physicalist has a lot of explaining to do. Answers to this argument include things like the idea that she doesn't gain knowledge, but acquaintance - though I must admit I see a plurality here: she comes to learn the experience of blueness, and she gains an acquaintance with. Conflating the two is counter-intuitive and needs further explanation to justify itself.
