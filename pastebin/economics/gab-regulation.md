A reminder: there is no such thing as an "unregulated" black market. Illegalization is itself the ultimate form of regulation, and its harsh effects are the logical end result of the usually more diffused ways in which the regulatory state facilitates economic centralization and hierarchy. The basic method of analysis employed by free-market anti-capitalists still works here: first, the strict barriers to entry that obtain in the criminalization of either producing, distributing, or consuming a particular good or service concentrates the emerging "black" industry into the hands of a relative few who are able and willing to overcome or absorb the artifical risks and costs that now exist; secondly,  the monopoly profits that its new criminal owners are able to extract from the risk premium and controls on general distribution grant them disproportionate gains in wealth (and thus power) compared to less privileged participants, and this facilitates the structures of hierarchy and violent domination that we see exacerbated in, e.g., the Mexican drug cartels; third, the State monopoly on the provision of law and security more broadly also includes the unique power of exclusion, so while at the same time being disabled from being able to provide "our" own competing institutions of justice black market participants are excluded from the full legal protections of any half-functional legal system, so market actors are heavily constrained in their options to bed against risk. Cartelization is heavily encouraged not just by the enforced lack of legal security, but a positive need to secure themselves against state legal enforcement. I could hardly detail all the intricate ways in which the nature of black markets are artifically shaped by various government actions in this single text, so it will suffice if the general point is understood. I should also note that I have in mind a particular emphasis on illegal drug markets and the ideological climates of the drug prohibition debate, since this is where the particular issue at hand is raised the most.

We've all seen this mantra, "legalize it, regulate it, tax it"—notice here the awkward dialectical nature of the statists' ideology: on one hand, they must construct the fantasy in your mind that some sovereign is necessary to function as an overseer that prevents things from getting too hot, and yet what they are essentially calling for is to immediately recall the heavy-handed actions of that same overseer in order to provide with a relatively looser, more flexible structure in which to use its regulatory powers. While it's not a logically impossible position, we should be suspicious when they agree on the need to immediately abolish the heaviest regulation in existence, general prohibition, to then reflexively insist on the need for "moderate" and "sensible" regulations. That is, while too much state prohibition and heavy-handedness does indeed nurture the most exaggerated examples of violence and hierarchy imaginable, tempered regulation not only does not proporitionally result in tempered hierarchy but it, so your typical progressive liberal claims, instead acts in the opposite way: it becomes a countervailing power to the oppressive functions of ubridled market mechanisms. Again, this is not an impossible position, but it does seem quite suspicious on its surface—it will require a nuanced explanation on why legal-regulationist measures do not simply foster a less extreme scenarios of the one described in the first paragraph.

But I wouldn't hold my breath—the kind of political ideology we are being hit with here is not a very serious one. It's better to instead think of it as a kind of post-political post-ideology, one where the existing structures of power are taken as a given and the whole of existence itself is to be subsumed under it—nothing can exist wholey self-contained, it either must be destroyed or absorbed into the existing system. Every action and every thing is either planned or regulated, and from that there exists only the spectrum of how aggressive or how tempered we should be with state controls. After all, what's the alternative? This is the final political form. This is the end of history; if you don't like it, then too bad because you certainly can't leave it.
