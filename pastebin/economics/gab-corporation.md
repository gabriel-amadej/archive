The hierarchical corporation violates all freed-market principles in a manner that is quite similar to the bureaucracy of a 'socialist' state. Its managers are managers of other people's money rather than their own; its substituted administrative incentives separate effort and productivity are separate from reward. People act more effectively when they fully internalize the positive and negative results of their actions, and they are more careful and efficient in spending their own money than they are when spending other people's money. The value of idiosyncratic knowledge is being lost here as well.

Contrary to the rhetoric of liberals, conservatives, and right-libertarians, corporations are entirely a product of numerous state interventions and subsidies.

But in order to adequately trace the sewing seeds of its existence, we must first touch upon the historical process of private accumulation: the land stolen from peasants in the early modern period—i.e. through the nullification of tenure rights, the enclosures of open fields—which served to transfer the so-called property of feudal obligarchies into a freehold right. These exproproiations drove off the majority of peasants off the land and deprived them of their independent access to the means of production, forcing them into what would now be indentifiable as the wage-labor system, all simultaneous with their effect of concentrating these former lands into the hands of the plutocracy.

In the industrial revolution, more wealth was concentrated into the hands of the ownership classes through the state's coercive restrictions on free association and the bargaining freedom of the labouring classes.

Now the state—what with its licensing and capitalization requirements for banks, reducing competition involved in supplying credit and driving up its price; with its monopolizing enforcement of property titles to vacant land; with its subsidization of economic centralization serving to increase the capital-intensiveness of production—serves to restrict labour's access to capital such that workers must sell their labour in a buyer's market, i.e. workers compete for jobs instead of the other way around, while the accommodating interventions reduce the number of employers competing for labour; and regulatory barriers to market entry hinder the ability of small property-owners to pool their capital together and construct competing businesses.

This legacy of state-enforced unequal exchange and state-assisted robbery, and its resulting monstrosity that is the hierarchical corporation, should not be confused with the advent of private associations that are constituted in fully voluntary, natural market conditions, and all the benefits it carries due to, e.g., the fact that they internalize all of the benefits and costs of their production decisions and such.
